The lighthouse at Point Reyes was built in 1870 on a ledge 300 feet below the top of the headland, because the fog that made the light necessary usually sat higher up the cliff. Keepers reached it by a stairway of 308 steps, and for decades the station log recorded the number of hours the fog signal ran each year; the highest total, 2,360 hours in 1887, is still quoted in the visitor centre.

The lens is a first-order Fresnel lens made in Paris in 1867, with 24 vertical panels and more than a thousand prisms. It floated on a bed of mercury and was turned by a clockwork mechanism driven by a weight that a keeper had to wind every two hours through the night. The characteristic was one flash every five seconds, visible about 24 nautical miles out to sea in clear weather.

Electricity reached the station in 1938, and the oil lamp was replaced by a 1,000-watt bulb. The original lens was retired in 1975 when an automated beacon was installed on a lower platform, and the old tower was opened to visitors two years later. The Coast Guard transferred the buildings to the National Park Service, which now maintains the lens as a museum object and runs the clockwork on demonstration days.

Two features make the site unusual among west-coast lights. First, it is one of the windiest places on the Pacific coast: gusts of 133 miles per hour were recorded in 1961, and the stairway is closed whenever sustained winds exceed 40 miles per hour. Second, the fog signal building sits at a different level from the lens room, which meant that for much of the station's history two keepers were on duty at once, one at each level, communicating by a speaking tube.

Visitors today walk down the same 308 steps, and the park estimates that about 1,600 people a day make the descent in summer. The stairway was rebuilt in 2019 with steel treads, and the lens room was reglazed the same year after storm damage to three of the original panes.
